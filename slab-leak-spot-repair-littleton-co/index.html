When the rest of the pipe is healthy, a small, precise opening in the slab is often the fastest and least expensive fix.

A spot repair — also called a direct-access repair — means opening the concrete right above the leak, replacing the damaged section of pipe, and restoring the slab. With accurate detection, the opening is usually about two feet square. It is the most direct way to stop a single slab leak, and on a basement slab under carpet it is often the cheapest option available.

When is a spot repair the right choice?

  • The home has had no previous slab leaks
  • The pipe around the leak is in good condition when exposed
  • The leak sits under carpet, bare concrete or a garage floor, not finished tile or hardwood
  • The line is not deeply buried under a footing or wall

If those don't apply, a reroute or epoxy lining may be better long-term value.

How does a direct-access slab repair work?

  1. Pinpoint the leak with acoustic and thermal tools so the opening goes in the right place.
  2. Protect the room. Flooring is pulled back carefully and plastic containment goes up to control concrete dust.
  3. Saw-cut the slab with a water-cooled saw to keep dust down, then break out the section.
  4. Excavate the soil to expose the pipe.
  5. Inspect the exposed pipe. If it is thin or pitted beyond the leak, we stop and tell you before continuing.
  6. Replace the damaged section with new pipe and approved couplings.
  7. Pressure-test the repair.
  8. Backfill and compact, then re-pour the concrete flush with the existing floor.

What should you expect during the repair?

Noise and dust

Cutting concrete is loud for a short while. Wet cutting and containment keep dust out of the rest of the house.

Water shut-off

You'll be without water on the affected line for a few hours. We restore service as soon as the repair is tested.

Concrete cure time

New concrete needs a few days to cure before carpet goes back and longer before tile or hardwood. We'll give you exact timing for your flooring.

Are there downsides to a spot repair?

It fixes one leak. If the pipe is corroding along its length, another leak can appear nearby within a few years. That is why we inspect the exposed pipe and tell you honestly what we see. If two leaks have already happened, we will usually steer you toward a reroute.

What are the warning signs of a slab leak?

Most homeowners call us because of one of these, often two at once:

  • A water bill that jumps with no change in habits, or a water meter that keeps spinning with every fixture off
  • A warm or hot patch on tile, vinyl or carpet — the classic sign of a hot-water line leak
  • The sound of running water when nothing is on, often loudest at night
  • Damp or musty carpet, lifting laminate, cupping hardwood or efflorescence (white crust) on a basement floor
  • New cracks in the slab or in drywall near the floor
  • Low water pressure at one fixture or across the house
  • A water heater that runs constantly, or a sudden jump in your gas bill

What do we look for once the pipe is exposed?

Opening the slab gives us a rare look at the pipe itself, and it tells us a lot about what comes next.

  • Wall thickness. If the copper near the leak is thin or pitted, more leaks are likely nearby.
  • Cause of the hole. A smooth worn spot points to abrasion against concrete or gravel; a green crusty pinhole points to corrosion from inside.
  • Bedding. Pipe resting on rock or rebar should be re-bedded in sand during the repair.
  • Sleeving. Where pipe passes through concrete without a protective sleeve, we add one.

We photograph everything and show you. If what we see changes our recommendation, we stop and talk it through before going further.

How is the concrete restored?

  1. Backfill in layers, compacting each so the new concrete has firm support.
  2. Re-bed and protect the pipe with sand and, where needed, a sleeve.
  3. Drill and pin the edges of the opening so the new concrete bonds to the old.
  4. Pour and finish flush with the existing slab.
  5. Let it cure before flooring goes back down.

Spot repairs in garages vs basements

Garage slabs are thicker in some homes and often hold the main water line where it enters the house. Repairs there are usually straightforward because there's no flooring to remove, though you'll want to keep cars off the patch for several days.

Basement slabs are thinner and often finished. The key there is containment — plastic sheeting and negative air keep concrete dust from spreading into the rest of the house.

How to prepare your home

  • Clear furniture and stored items from around the marked area
  • Point out any floor heat, wiring or sprinkler lines you know of
  • Locate spare tiles or planks if you have them
  • Plan for a few hours without water on the affected line
Do you haul away the concrete?

Yes. Broken concrete and excess soil are removed and the area is cleaned.

When we'll recommend against a spot repair

We'll steer you away from opening the slab if the home has already had slab leaks, if the pipe we expect to find is original copper 40+ years old, if the leak sits under a large tile or hardwood area, or if the slab may be post-tensioned. In those cases a reroute or lining usually costs about the same or less once flooring is counted — and lasts longer.

Spot repair cost scenarios

A basement leak under carpet with sound pipe and an easy opening sits near the low end of our range. The same leak under kitchen tile, with cabinets nearby and a deeper line, lands toward the top — before tile replacement. That spread is why we price your home specifically after detection.

How we keep a spot repair clean

Concrete work doesn't have to mean a dusty house. We cut wet to control dust, seal the work area with plastic sheeting and zipper doors, and run air filtration where needed. Soil and broken concrete go out in covered containers along a protected path, and at the end of the job we vacuum and wipe down the area. Most homeowners are surprised at how contained the work stays.

Is the repaired section stronger than before?

The new section of pipe is new material, bedded in clean sand and often sleeved where it passes through concrete. That's better protection than the original installation had, which is part of why we take the extra steps.

Spot repair FAQ

How big is the hole in my floor?

Usually around 2 ft by 2 ft. It can be larger if the pipe is deep or the leak location is less certain.

Will the patched concrete be level?

Yes. We finish the new concrete flush with the existing slab so flooring lays flat.

Can you match my tile?

We replace the concrete; tile matching depends on whether spare tiles exist. Many homeowners keep extra tiles in the garage — worth checking.

How long until I can use the room?

You can walk on it within a day. Flooring timing depends on the concrete cure and the flooring type.

Is a spot repair permanent?

The repaired section is new. The rest of the line is the same age it was before, so long-term durability depends on that pipe's condition.

Can you do a spot repair in my garage?

Yes. Garage slabs often hold the main line, and repairs there are usually simple because there's no flooring to remove.

What if you find more corrosion once the slab is open?

We stop, show you photos and explain your options — including closing up and rerouting instead — before continuing.

Does a spot repair need a permit?

Requirements vary by jurisdiction. We'll tell you whether your address needs one before work starts.

Slab leak right now?

Live answer, day or night. A technician with acoustic and thermal gear can usually be on the way the same day.
