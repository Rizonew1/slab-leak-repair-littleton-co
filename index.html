What is a slab leak?

A slab leak is a leak in a water or drain line that runs beneath or inside a concrete floor. In Littleton that floor is often a basement slab, a garage slab or the lower level of a split-level, as well as true slab-on-grade ranches and townhomes. Because the pipe is buried, the leak can run for weeks before you notice anything — and by then water may have soaked into the soil, the flooring or the walls.

There are two main kinds:

  • Supply-line leaks — pressurized hot or cold water lines. These raise your water bill and, for hot lines, often cause warm spots on the floor.
  • Drain-line leaks — waste and sewer pipes under the slab. These don't raise the water bill but can cause odors, damp floors and settling.

How do you know if you have a slab leak?

Most homeowners call us because of one of these, often two at once:

  • A water bill that jumps with no change in habits, or a water meter that keeps spinning with every fixture off
  • A warm or hot patch on tile, vinyl or carpet — the classic sign of a hot-water line leak
  • The sound of running water when nothing is on, often loudest at night
  • Damp or musty carpet, lifting laminate, cupping hardwood or efflorescence (white crust) on a basement floor
  • New cracks in the slab or in drywall near the floor
  • Low water pressure at one fixture or across the house
  • A water heater that runs constantly, or a sudden jump in your gas bill
Free 10-minute test: turn off every fixture, then watch the small leak dial on your water meter for 10–15 minutes. If it moves, and toilets and outdoor faucets aren't leaking, you probably have a hidden leak.

Why do slab leaks happen in Littleton homes?

Littleton's housing stock tells most of the story. A large share of homes went up during the building boom of the 1960s through the 1980s, and many of them have copper water lines buried in or under a concrete floor — a basement slab, a garage slab, or the slab of a ranch or split-level that sits on grade. Newer subdivisions on the south and west sides were often plumbed with PEX, which fails differently but can still be damaged under concrete.

  • Expansive clay soil. Much of the Front Range sits on clay that swells when wet and shrinks when dry. That seasonal movement pushes and pulls on pipes locked in concrete, and fittings and bends take the strain first.
  • Abrasion. A copper line that rests against gravel, rebar or the edge of the slab vibrates every time water flows. Over decades it rubs a hole through its own wall.
  • Water chemistry and age. Pinhole corrosion from the inside is common in older copper. Once one pinhole appears, others along the same run often follow.
  • Freeze-thaw and construction defects. Lines that run near an exterior wall, a crawl-space transition or a poorly bedded trench are more exposed to stress.

What drives the price up or down?

  • Floor covering. Carpet is cheap to pull back; tile and hardwood are not.
  • Depth and location. Lines under footings, cabinets or deep fill take more work.
  • Pipe condition. Corroded pipe pushes toward reroute or repipe.
  • Permits. Many jurisdictions require them for reroutes and repipes.
  • Time of day. After-hours emergency visits may carry an extra fee — we tell you on the phone first.

Does insurance cover slab leaks in Colorado?

Many homeowners policies cover sudden water damage and the cost to access the pipe — breaking and replacing the floor — but not the pipe repair itself. Some cap slab-leak coverage. We give you photos, readings and a written report showing where the leak was and what caused it, which is what adjusters ask for. Always check your own policy.

How to reduce your risk of a slab leak

  • Know your shutoff. Find and tag your main valve now.
  • Watch your meter. Check the leak dial every few months.
  • Manage water pressure. Very high pressure stresses old pipe; a pressure-reducing valve may help.
  • Keep water away from the foundation. Good grading and downspouts help keep clay soil stable.
  • Plan ahead in older homes. If your copper is 40+ years old, budget for a reroute or repipe before the next leak forces it.

Slab leak myths we hear often

  • "Slab leaks always mean jackhammering." Reroutes and lining often avoid it entirely.
  • "If I don't see water, it's not serious." Most slab leaks drain into the soil and stay invisible for weeks.
  • "Newer homes don't get slab leaks." They do, though less often and usually for different reasons.
  • "Insurance pays for everything." Many policies cover damage and access, not the pipe repair itself.

How do I know if I have a slab leak?

The most reliable early sign is a water meter that moves with every fixture off. Others include a warm spot on the floor, running-water sounds, damp flooring and higher bills.

Can you fix a slab leak without breaking the floor?

Often, yes. A reroute runs new pipe through walls and ceilings, and epoxy lining repairs the pipe from the inside.

Is a slab leak an emergency?

If water is surfacing or you can't stop it, yes — shut off the main and call. Otherwise it's still urgent, because damage builds every day.
